Six segments, one circle. Software that is built for everyone.

Why SE4All, and why now

Software decides who can learn, work, bank, get care, and take part in public life. A large share of it still shuts out people with disabilities, older adults, and anyone facing a situational or cognitive barrier.

At the same time, AI has moved into the heart of how software is built and used. It writes interface code, powers conversational agents, and increasingly makes decisions on a user's behalf. That is both a risk and an opening. The same models that can describe an image, caption a video, simplify dense text, or restructure a cluttered page are the tools that could close accessibility gaps at a scale that manual work has never reached, but only if we engineer and evaluate them with that goal in mind.

SE4All treats this as a software engineering problem. How do we capture accessibility in requirements, test and audit for it, build tools and pipelines around it, share the work between people and AI, and hold the results to account? The workshop keeps its feet in software engineering methodology and practice while drawing on the accessibility, HCI, and AI communities.

The timing is deliberate. The EU Accessibility Act is now in force, WCAG 3.0 is taking shape, and ICSE 2027 is hosted in Dublin, at the heart of the European policy landscape driving demand for accessible software.

What we want from the day

We want people to leave with new collaborators and a clear sense of what to work on next. Alongside paper presentations, the programme makes room for short practitioner talks and facilitated breakout groups. The notes from those groups feed a short community research roadmap that we circulate to everyone who attends.

We especially welcome experience reports and contributions from engineers and researchers with lived experience of disability. Their perspective keeps the work grounded in the needs of the people it is meant to serve, and we would rather build that in from the first edition than add it later.

Topics of interest

Read broadly. A paper need not be about disability in the narrow sense to fit, as long as it speaks to software engineering and AI for accessibility and inclusion. Open a topic to see what it covers.

AI across the lifecycle

Generating, repairing, reviewing, and testing software, and what it means for accessibility and inclusion.

Human and AI together

Human in the loop workflows and crowdsourcing for building and maintaining better software.

Testing and analysis

Automated testing, auditing, and quality assurance for accessibility, usability, and inclusive design.

Process and design

Requirements, architecture, design, and maintenance that treat inclusion as a first class concern.

Empirical studies

Mining repositories, datasets, and benchmarks for inclusive software engineering.

Emerging interfaces

Accessibility of conversational agents, generated UIs, mobile, web, IoT, and smart environments.

Trust and fairness

Verification, dependability, fairness, and responsible engineering of AI enabled software for diverse users.

Human and social aspects

Diversity, equity, standards and policy in practice (WCAG, EU Accessibility Act, Section 508), and education.

Experience reports

Industrial case studies and lessons learned from building software for all.

If you are unsure whether your work fits, write to us and ask.

Submissions

Two tracks, IEEE format, single blind review. Every paper receives at least three reviews from the programme committee. Accepted papers appear in the ICSE 2027 Companion Proceedings, published by IEEE.

8pages max

Regular papers

Original research, empirical studies, and tool papers, including references.

5pages max

Short papers

Work in progress, position and vision papers, emerging ideas, tool demonstrations, and industrial experience reports, including references.

FormatPDF, IEEE Conference Proceedings template, 10 pt, IEEEtran.
ReviewSingle blind. At least three reviews, judged on novelty, relevance, soundness, clarity, and the discussion it can spark.
Ideas welcomePosition and emerging ideas papers are judged on the strength of the idea, not on how complete the results are.
